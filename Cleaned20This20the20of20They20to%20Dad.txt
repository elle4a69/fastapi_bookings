BUT WAIT
--------

Fentanyl’s primary purpose is the relief of uncontrollable pain.
Midazolam’s primary purpose is sedation for uncontrollable, severe terminal agitation and restlessness.
Dad had neither of those symptoms when they administered this medication. He was in pain—his stomach was hurting—but they had not exhausted the available options for managing it. There was no agitation, severe restlessness, breathlessness, or anything of that nature.
I do not need to be a doctor to know that much more could have been done to manage his pain. There was a long way to go before it could reasonably have been described as uncontrollable. Even if he had become agitated—which he had not—the first question should have been why. Under the circumstances, I believe he was extremely calm.
In any event, should they not have addressed his pain first? That is what I believe making someone comfortable means: identify the pain and bring it under control. At that time, his pain was under control. He was not breathless or struggling for air. There was no reason whatsoever to administer a sedative intended to render him unconscious, or to attempt to do so.
Yes, I understand the argument about the doctrine of double effect, but the fundamental point is that, when they gave him that medication, he did not need it. That is not to say he might not have needed it one, two or three days later—or that he would ever have needed it at all. If he had become distressed, unable to breathe or otherwise severely symptomatic, I would have been the first person to call a nurse or doctor and ask them to give him something to relieve that distress.
But we were not at that point. We may never have reached it.
The only reason he later became breathless was, in my firm belief, the medication they gave him. His lungs had been fine. He did not have pneumonia. There had been no rattling or other abnormal sounds when he breathed. There was nothing of that nature at all.
I believe he stopped breathing because the medication suppressed his central nervous system. There was otherwise no apparent reason for his breathing to stop. I also challenge the suggestion that renal failure caused, or materially contributed to, his death. I did not see jaundice or any other visible indication of kidney or liver failure. I have seen enough people die to recognise the difference. I saw none of those signs.
What I witnessed was his breathing becoming slower and slower, followed by his heart rate slowing, until his breathing eventually stopped. That is what happened. I believe they killed him, and I will say that plainly, regardless of what anyone else says: they killed him.
His remaining time may only have amounted to days, but those days belonged to him. They were his to spend as he chose, and he wanted to go home. That is where he should have been—with his family, in his own home.
Who is best placed to describe how my father died? I am, because I was there every minute—throughout every day and every night. There were no doctors constantly present. There was nobody else there apart from my mother and me. I witnessed how he died.
I do not ordinarily wish suffering upon anyone. But the doctors who prescribed that medication should understand exactly what they subjected my father to. What happened to him was profoundly wrong, and they must be held accountable for it.
From what I understand now, midazolam and fentanyl together suppress the central nervous system. They slow the breathing, making it shallow. They dull the body, quieten it, bury it, and force it down and down and down.
And when breathing fails, everything else follows.
The body starts retreating from itself.

The feet go cold.

I watched the life retreat from my father.

This is me refusing to let my father disappear behind words like “comfort” and “protocol” and “peaceful”.
Because I was there.
I watched him.
I held him.
I felt him fight.
And then, on the very same day his life was taken from him, we went home and I watched him being erased from the house.
His seat at the table was taken.
His armchair was taken.
His bed was taken.
His name was taken.
Even his funeral felt hijacked.
And I was meant to be okay with all of that.
Well, I am not.
I was not okay with it then, and I am not okay with it now.
So everyone can yell and scream at me all they like. I really do not care anymore. I have been told recently that people do not like the way I speak. That I am condescending. That I am too rigid. Too set in my ways.
But what does that really mean?
Does it mean that when I see something as black, and everyone else insists it is white, I am supposed to betray myself and call it white just to keep the peace?
I am not going to do that.
The reason people do not like hearing what I have to say is simple.
People do not like the truth.
People hate the truth when it hurts.
But I am not going to bury the truth just because it makes someone else uncomfortable.
I have been silenced for too many years.
And in a way, I feel like I am being silenced the same way Dad was silenced in his death.
Everyone else can believe what they want to believe.
I am allowed to believe what I believe.
But apparently, I am not allowed to say it out loud.
Well, now I have.
And maybe this is bigger than Dad. Maybe this is the thing people have always hated about me. I question things. I do not automatically accept the official story just because it is handed to me by someone in authority. I do not bow my head because a doctor, a government, a newsreader, or anyone else tells me not to look too closely.
I have never been able to do that.
People have mocked me for the things I question.
They mock me when I say I do not believe what we were told about 9/11.
They mock me when I question whether we were told the truth about the moon landing.
They mock me when I challenge the story of the earth as a spinning ball.
They mock me when I say I had concerns about the vaccines from the beginning.
They mock me when I say I thought Maui looked like a land grab.
Maybe I have been wrong about things.
Maybe I have been right about things.
Maybe some of what I question sounds outrageous to other people.
But that is not the point.
The point is that I am willing to look.
I am willing to ask.
I am willing to stand there, even if everyone laughs, and say, “This does not make sense to me.”
And if I am wrong, I have the courage to change my mind and say I was wrong.
But I will not pretend I believe something when I do not.
I will not call something normal when it feels wrong in every part of me.
I will not let people shame me into silence because my questions make them uncomfortable.
So for the first time in my life, maybe I am being selfish enough to say out loud what I have been carrying inside.
Maybe I am finally putting it somewhere instead of swallowing it.
And if what I feel is wrong, then so be it.
But it is what I feel.
It is what I saw.
It is what I believe.
And I am done apologising for having the courage to say it.
